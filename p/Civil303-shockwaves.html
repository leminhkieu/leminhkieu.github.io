Why this matters

A crash that lasts fifteen minutes may block a motorway for hours

Phantom traffic jam

Traffic congestion is not still, but a wave that travels backwards through the traffic stream, usually faster than you would guess, and it keeps travelling, even after the thing that caused it has gone.

What can we do if we can estimate these waves?

Start by watching the road

A truck breaks down and blocks one lane of a three-lane motorway. Look at what happens to the vehicles behind it.

Every vehicle in the picture is still moving forwards. The dashed line, the back of the queue, moves backwards.

What is a shockwave?

Definition

A shockwave is the moving boundary between two different traffic states. On one side of the boundary the traffic has one flow, density and speed. On the other side it has different ones.
The boundary is not static but it has speed, that we'll calculate as Traffic Engineers!

By-product of traffic congestion: every change in traffic conditions creates a shockwave.

This is why we also call them kinematic waves. The physics is the same as a wave in shallow water or a pressure wave in a gas pipe, and it comes from the same place: nothing is created and nothing disappears.

Speed of the shockwaves

\[ \omega_{AB} \;=\; \frac{q_B - q_A}{k_B - k_A} \qquad \text{km/h} \]

A is the state traffic is coming from. B is the state it is going into.

$\omega < 0$wave travels backwards, up the queue
|
$\omega = 0$wave stands still at a fixed bottleneck
|
$\omega > 0$wave travels forwards, with the traffic

Finding the states: the Greenshields curve

Greenshields assumed speed falls off in a straight line as the road fills up:

\[ v = v_f\left(1 - \frac{k}{k_j}\right) \]

Multiply by $k$ and you get the familiar bell-shaped curve of flow against density:

\[ q = v_f\left(k - \frac{k^2}{k_j}\right) \]

Our three traffic states

The truck blocks a lane at 08:00 and is towed away at 08:10. Before we can calculate anything, we name the states.

Shockwave speed is just the slope of the line joining two states on this curve, so you can read both waves off the diagram before doing any algebra.

08:00 to 08:10   the queue forms

Traffic goes from A into B, so:

\[ \omega_{AB} = \frac{1440 - 3000}{180 - 50} = -12 \ \text{km/h} \]

The minus sign means backwards. The red line on the diagram is the back of the queue, and it slopes down and to the right because it is moving upstream.

After 10 minutes it has reached

\[ 12 \times \tfrac{10}{60} = 2 \ \text{km} \]

08:10   the truck is towed away

The front of the queue now moves off at capacity, so traffic goes from B into C:

\[ \omega_{BC} = \frac{4000 - 1440}{100 - 180} = -32 \ \text{km/h} \]

Also backwards, and much faster than the wave behind it. The green line is steeper than the red one.

The truck has gone, but the back of the queue is still growing. Drivers arriving from upstream have no idea the road is clear.

08:16   the two waves meet

The green wave is closing on the red one at $32 - 12 = 20$ km/h, across a 2 km gap:

\[ \frac{2}{20} = 0.1 \ \text{h} = 6 \ \text{min} \]

So they meet 16 minutes in, at: $12 \times \tfrac{16}{60} = 3.2 \ \text{km}$

Check it the other way: $32 \times 0.1 = 3.2$ km. The two waves must agree, and they do.

That meeting point is the longest the queue ever gets. The last driver to be stopped is standing there.

08:26   back to normal

Once the queue is gone, traffic at the meeting point goes from C back to A. That is a third wave:

\[ \omega_{CA} = \frac{3000 - 4000}{50 - 100} = +20 \ \text{km/h} \]

Positive, so this one runs forwards, back towards the site. It covers the 3.2 km in

\[ \tfrac{3.2}{20} \times 60 = 9.6 \ \text{min} \]

The site is finally back to normal at 25.6 minutes. A ten minute breakdown impacted the motorway for over 25 minute.

The whole story on one time-space diagram

Grey lines are individual vehicles. Watch one: it runs in steeply, flattens almost to nothing inside the queue, then picks up again once the green wave passes it.

What those ten minutes cost

Recovery took 26 minutes for a 10 minute blockage. Roughly two and a half times the incident duration.

Where the data come from?

Arriving traffic, A: Measured directly. Loop detectors or tube counts give the flow. Put that flow into the Greenshields equation and take the free-flowing root for density.
The queue, B: Set by what the blocked section can pass, not by demand. Take that flow and use the congested root for density.
Moving off, C: Always capacity, $q_c = v_f k_j / 4$ at $k_c = k_j/2$. A queue discharges as fast as the road allows.
